GT Movies Store

A Django storefront for browsing movies, reviewing them, and buying them.

  • Course CS 2340
  • Stack Django, Python, SQLite
  • Time About 10 hours

Demo

A walkthrough of the whole application: creating an account, browsing the catalog, writing a review, and placing an order.

What it does

Each screen in the application exists to satisfy a specific user story. Here is what I built and who it is for.

Sign up and log in

As a visitor, I want an account so that my cart and my past orders belong to me.

Registration and login run on Django's built-in authentication, with the account app handling the forms and session. The navigation bar changes once you are signed in, and the cart and orders pages are closed to anonymous visitors.

Browse and search the catalog

As a shopper, I want to see what is for sale and find a specific title quickly.

The home page lists every movie with its cover, title, and price, pulled from the Movie model. A search box filters the list by title on the server side, so the results are a queryset rather than anything client-side.

Movie detail and reviews

As a shopper, I want to read what other people thought, and leave my own review that I can edit or delete later.

Each movie has a detail page with its description, price, and every review left on it. Signed-in users can post a review, and the edit and delete controls only appear on reviews they wrote — the view checks the review's author against the request user before it will do anything.

Cart

As a shopper, I want to collect a few movies and see what the total comes to before I commit.

The cart lives in the session, keyed by movie id and quantity. The cart page renders each line item with a running total and lets you remove a single title or clear the whole thing.

Checkout and order history

As a shopper, I want a record of what I bought.

Checkout writes an Order with its related Items, empties the session cart, and sends you to a confirmation. The orders page lists every order on the account with its date and total.

How I worked

I worked backwards from the user stories rather than forwards from the code. Before writing anything I listed every story the specification asked for, decided which model each one needed to touch, and grouped them into apps — accounts, movies, cart. That list became my checklist, and I did not move on from a story until it worked end to end in the browser.

Within each story I kept the same order: model first, then migration, then the view, then the template, then the URL to reach it. Doing it in that order meant I was never writing a template against a field that did not exist yet. I committed after each working story, so when something broke I could tell exactly which story broke it.

Most of my time went to the parts where state has to survive between requests: the session-based cart, and making sure a user can only edit their own reviews. When I got stuck it was usually a mismatch between what the template expected and what the view was passing, and the Django error page plus a few print statements in the view resolved almost all of it. For the rest I went back to the Django documentation on querysets and relationships rather than pattern-matching from tutorials, which was slower at the time and much faster overall.

If I picked this up again, the first thing I would change is the cart. Keeping it in the session was the right call for a ten-hour build, but it means the cart vanishes when the session does. Moving it into a model attached to the user would fix that and make the checkout logic simpler.
